# Rasmussen's Abstraction Hierarchy and Its Descendants: Research Report

## 1. The five levels

Rasmussen introduced the hierarchy in "The role of hierarchical knowledge representation in decisionmaking and system management" (IEEE Trans. SMC-15, 1985, https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6313353/) and *Information Processing and Human-Machine Interaction* (1986), generalizing protocol studies of electronics technicians and plant operators troubleshooting. Vicente and Rasmussen's 1992 EID paper gives the canonical compact definitions (https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/158017888/SMC.PDF):

- **Functional Purpose**: "the purposes for which the system was designed."
- **Abstract Function**: "the intended causal structure of the process in terms of mass, energy, information, or value flows." In practice this level holds the governing principles and priority measures (conservation laws, safety-vs-efficiency trade-offs) against which lower levels are judged.
- **Generalized Function**: "the basic functions that the plant is designed to achieve," described device-independently (e.g. "heating", "storage", "transport").
- **Physical Function**: "the characteristics of the components and the connections between them," i.e. what specific classes of equipment can do (electric motor vs combustion engine).
- **Physical Form**: "the appearance and spatial location of those components." Rasmussen (1986, p. 17): "the lowest, most concrete level of abstraction is the representation of physical form, i.e. the physical appearance and configuration of the system and its parts."

The same paper is explicit that five is a process-control finding, not a law: "The exact number of levels and their content will vary from domain to domain as a function of the different types of constraints inherent in each work domain. For example, five levels of constraint have been found to be useful for describing process control systems." Lintern (2009, *Foundations and Pragmatics of CWA*, http://www.cognitivesystemsdesign.net/Downloads/Foundations%20&%20Pragmatics%20of%20CWA%20(Lintern2009).pdf) renames the levels for non-process domains as Domain Purpose, Domain Values and Priorities, Domain Functions, Physical Functions, Physical Objects, noting "considerable variation in the literature regarding the labels given to the top three levels" and that "the upper three levels point to intentional properties while the lower two levels point to physical properties."

**Non-control worked examples.** The best-documented one is Pejtersen's BookHouse fiction-retrieval system for public libraries, developed from a CWA/means-ends analysis of reference interviews (Rasmussen, Pejtersen & Goodstein, *Cognitive Systems Engineering*, 1994; Fidel's summary at http://faculty.washington.edu/fidelr/RayaPubs/CWA-bookchapter.pdf). A fully spelled-out hospital example is St-Maurice & Burns's AH for patient treatment with medical records (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5554354/): purposes = patient education, financial compensation, health improvement, sustainable care, public safety; abstract functions = patient values, best-practice guidelines, resource limits, risk/cost/outcome balancing, information flow; generalized functions = clinical patient-flow and information-flow processes; physical functions = patients, assessments, staff, forms, records; physical form = their attributes. The authors note "there are many ways to model abstract concepts and ideas" and split the model into two views to manage scope. Lintern's tutorial example is a hobby carpenter's workshop. An informal but clear software example (a deployment system, "Managed Delivery") is at https://surfingcomplexity.blog/2022/09/17/up-and-down-the-abstraction-hierarchy/.

## 2. The Abstraction-Decomposition Space

The ADS crosses the abstraction axis with a part-whole axis (whole system, subsystems, units, components). Vicente (1999) holds that every cell is a complete but different description of the same domain; Lintern (p. 31): "In principle, it should be possible to populate every cell of this two-dimensional space but in practice, only the cells that follow the upper right to lower left diagonal provide useful information for design. The lower left cell offers the most evocative example ... the physical object at a whole system level of description is a hobby carpenter's workshop. While that is a valid description, it does not provide useful information." Purpose is meaningful at whole-system grain; physical form is meaningful at component grain. Ekberg's critical-infrastructure ADS (https://securityanddefence.pl/pdf-146789-82662?filename=Abstraction_decomposition.pdf) repeats: "It is often not necessary to populate the whole table since the solution is often found along the diagonal."

Lintern adds: "There is no rule to specify which of the cells need to be populated ... It is not even necessary to populate cells in every row. In some design problems, you may be concerned with conceptual design rather than physical implementation and might therefore be interested only in the top three rows." He also notes there is "no specified number for degrees of decomposition"; it is set by what domain experts find useful (tools need not be decomposed for furniture-making, but must be for tool repair).

**How the two link types interact.** Means-ends links run vertically between abstraction levels; part-whole (containment) links run horizontally within a level. Lintern's Appendix A stresses the asymmetry: "The decomposition dimension conforms to the property of containment" but the abstraction dimension does not, since one lower-level node can serve several higher-level nodes. Lind (2003) attacks Rasmussen's diagram coupling the two axes along the main diagonal in diagnosis: "it is a strange coincidence that the number of levels along the two dimensions is the same" and "the coupling between the means-end levels and the whole-part levels is both dependent on the specific plant modeled and the end in view," so "there is in general a weak correlation between the means-end levels and the levels of whole-part decomposition" (https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/making-sense-of-the-abstraction-hierarchy-in-the-power-plant-doma/).

## 3. Why up, how down; many-to-many; adjacency

Leveson's NASA intent-specification report (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19990089302/downloads/19990089302.pdf) quotes Rasmussen directly: "in a means-ends abstraction, the current level specifies what, the level below how, and the level above why." Crucially the levels are emergent, not aggregative: "When moving from one level to the next higher level, the change in system properties represented is not merely removal of details of information on the physical or material properties. More fundamentally, information is added on higher-level principles governing the coordination of the various functions or elements at the lower level." Direction of propagation: "Changes in goals will propagate downward through the levels while changes in the physical resources (such as faults or failures) will propagate upward ... reasons for proper function are derived 'top-down.' In contrast, causes of improper function ... are explained 'bottom up.'"

**Many-to-many**: yes, by design. Leveson: "Mappings between levels are many-to-many: Components of the lower levels can serve several purposes while purposes at a higher level may be realized using several components." The INL nuclear WDA methodology (https://inldigitallibrary.inl.gov/sites/sti/sti/6439290.pdf) says the links reveal "complex mappings among the levels (many-to-one, many-to-many, one-to-many). Most items in all levels will usually have more than one link." Lintern (Appendix A) argues this does not make it a network: it is a stratified hierarchy in Mesarovic's sense that merely relaxes containment.

**Adjacent only**: the standard formulation. Vicente (1999, p. 7) defines means-ends links as "the relationship between adjacent levels in a means-ends hierarchy." Lintern: "a stratified hierarchy defined by means-ends relations between adjacent levels," with chains Physical Objects to Physical Functions to Domain Functions to Values to Purpose. Naikar (2013, p. 30) uses the same rule. I found no mainstream source permitting skip-level links; in practice analysts who need one insert an intermediate node. Lintern also insists links are *structural* (activity-independent), not action means-ends, and prefers a "Product-Function-Resource" reading over "Why-What-How" because "why" invites goal/task answers and "how" invites activity descriptions, both of which belong to later CWA phases.

## 4. Beyond process control: intentional domains and software

Vicente (1999) places domains on a continuum from **causal** (constrained by physical law) to **intentional** (constrained by actors' intentions, values, conventions). Hajdukiewicz, Burns, Vicente & Eggleston, "Work Domain Analysis for Intentional Systems" (HFES 1999, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/154193129904300343) state: "For causal systems, the environment is strongly constrained by the laws of nature. For intentional systems, the environment is strongly constrained by actors' intentions, values, and priorities of practice," and demonstrate on emergency ambulance dispatch and military command and control. The main adaptation is to reinterpret Abstract Function as **priorities and values** (Lintern's "Domain Values and Priorities"; Naikar's "values and priority measures"), and Generalized Function as "purpose-related functions." Reising (2000, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/154193120004400152) reviews this extension. Burns, Bryant & Chalmers (2005, IEEE SMC-A 35:603, https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1495604/) model a Halifax-class frigate, treating values as "a type of soft constraint" in a "multipurpose, loosely bound work domain." Naikar's defence work (Naikar & Sanderson 2001 airborne early warning; Naikar, Hopcroft & Moylan 2005 DSTO report https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA449707.pdf; Naikar 2013 *Work Domain Analysis: Concepts, Guidelines, and Cases*) supplies most of the current guidelines. Carden, Goode & Salmon extend the bottom level with **cognitive objects** (memes such as a 12-bar blues pattern) because intentional systems contain "objects that exist only at a conceptual level" (https://hf-sts.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/accounting-for-memes-in-sociotechnical-systems-extending-the-abstraction-hierarchy-to-1.pdf). A 2019 systematic review of 32 published AHs found 21 causal and 11 intentional, 22 with five levels, 11 industrial (Moïse et al., https://doi.org/10.1145/3366550.3372256).

**Software and information systems.** Direct applications include: Leveson's intent specifications (TSE 2000, http://sunnyday.mit.edu/papers/intent-tse.pdf), which explicitly contrast Rasmussen's means-ends abstraction with the aggregation and information-hiding abstractions of software engineering; BookHouse and later web-search studies (Fidel & Pejtersen); ecological interface design for computer network management (Burns, Kuo & Ng 2003, *Computer Networks*, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/223787710_Ecological_interface_design_A_new_approach_for_visualizing_network_management; Duez & Vicente 2005); Elm, Potter, Gualtieri, Roth & Easter's Applied CWA, whose Functional Abstraction Network drives decision-support software design (https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.1201/9780203489925-47/); and the "From CWA to Software Engineering" chapter in Stanton et al. (2017, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317404039_From_Cognitive_Work_Analysis_to_Software_Engineering_Applications_Extensions_and_Future_Directions). Leveson's own verdict on level count applies: "The exact number and content of the means-ends hierarchy levels may differ from domain to domain," and she "changed the content of the levels" for software (adding environment, operator, and interface models).

## 5. Criticisms

Lind's "Making sense of the abstraction hierarchy in the power plant domain" (CTW 2003, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10111-002-0109-4) is the sharpest. Conclusions: "the semantics of the means-end levels and their relations are vaguely defined"; "the commitment to a fixed number of levels of means-end abstractions should be abandoned and more attention given to the problem of level identification"; the AH is "example-based," with "no clear criteria to evaluate the adequacy and consistency of a model"; a user is "framed by the framework" and "tend[s] to regard the number of levels in the AH as paradigmatic." Specific problems: relations between different adjacent-level pairs are probably not the same kind of relation; many-to-one mappings are "inconsistent with basic principles of causal reasoning" (a cause must be necessary, but alternative means make it unnecessary); embedded control loops create circular means-ends structures; Rasmussen himself put a washing machine's purpose at Physical Function; and there are "no principles or rules of decomposition or composition on the level of the AH." He warns that clarifying the semantics "will invariably reduce the range of work domains where it can be applied." Lintern (2006) replied that most of this is semantic, and that the term "Abstraction Hierarchy is unsatisfactory because it encourages neglect of the decomposition dimension." Naikar's book addresses "should we always use five levels? What should those levels be named?" and argues several valid variants exist (INL summary). Burns, Bisantz & Roth (2004, *Human Factors* 46:711, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8024445_Lessons_From_a_Comparison_of_Work_Domain_Models_Representational_Choices_and_Their_Implications) compared independent models of the same C2 domain and found consistent purposes but "fundamental choices in modeling approach" differing between analysts. Miller & Sanderson (2000) found the AH could not handle biological processes with embedded control. The INL authors summarize: "in spite of the general acceptance of the principles, it has actually proven difficult to apply WDA in practice."

## 6. Recursive and nested use

No canonical rule exists; Lind flags this gap explicitly ("it is not obvious how a multiple of AH instances ... is combined in the representation of a superior level of activity"). Practitioners do it anyway:

- **Boundary objects**: Burns et al. (2005) and NASA's EVA WDA (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150020928/downloads/20150020928.pdf) split a large domain into boundary objects (EV crew, IV crew, Mission Control, environment), give each its own AH, and overlay them under shared overarching purposes.
- **System of systems**: Ekberg's critical-infrastructure ADS treats "the work domain ... as a system of systems," with sectors, sub-sectors, and entity types as the decomposition axis.
- **Living Systems Theory + AH** (CTW 2015, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10111-015-0338-y) stacks AHs across LST's eight organisational levels.
- Lintern nests decompositions graphically and describes activity "shaped by loosely coupled constraints between several levels of hierarchically nested contexts."
- Lind's Multilevel Flow Modeling solves the composition problem with "enabling conditions": a subsystem's function becomes a condition that is an objective of another activity, which is the closest thing to a formal "physical form here becomes functional purpose there" rule.

## 7. Other ends-means hierarchies

**Function-Behaviour-Structure (Gero 1990; situated FBS, Gero & Kannengiesser 2004).** Three ontological categories, F (what it is for), B (what it does; expected Be vs derived Bs), S (what it is), linked by eight processes (formulation, synthesis, analysis, evaluation, documentation, three reformulations). It is a single-level triad, not a scale ladder. Diertens' "Composition in the FBS Framework" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.00542) adds explicit recursion for software: the structure S and description D of model M generate the functions F_i of component models, each with its own FBS loop, and reformulation failures at a component propagate upward to reformulate F or S of the parent. So FBS composes only via that extension. (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function-Behaviour-Structure_ontology)

**Axiomatic Design (Suh).** Four domains (customer, functional, physical, process); design maps FRs to DPs via a design matrix subject to the Independence and Information axioms. Decomposition is by **zigzagging**: choose DPs at level k before decomposing FRs at level k+1, because "the design parameters stated on this top level influence the FRs of the secondary ... level" (MIT OCW chapter, https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/2-800-tribology-fall-2004/7956138e737f6cb1e9fb744fcc6c48ef_ch10_axiomatic.pdf). Inherently recursive across scales; Suh applied it to software (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/073658459190036R). Unlike the AH, it forbids many-to-many (coupled) mappings.

**QFD (Akao).** Four chained matrices: product planning (House of Quality), part deployment, process planning, production planning. "The 'hows' of one phase become the 'whats' of the next," keeping a customer want traceable to a shop-floor check (https://quality-one.com/qfd/). Composition across scales is literal: the chain can extend to system, subsystem, and component levels.

**Value-Focused Thinking (Keeney 1992).** Separates a *fundamental objectives hierarchy* (decomposition of ends) from a *means-ends objectives network* (many-to-many). Asking "why is that important?" moves up toward fundamental objectives; "how could you achieve it?" moves down. Lind cites Keeney as the sounder theory of means-ends semantics (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11295223/; Keeney 1996 EJOR https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0377221796000045).

**Alexander's pattern language (1977).** 253 patterns ordered by scale from region to construction detail; each pattern's prelude names the larger patterns it completes and its postscript names the smaller patterns needed to complete it. Salingaros ("The Structure of Pattern Languages", ARQ 2000, https://www.permacultureproject.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/The-Structure-of-Pattern-Languages-by-Nikos-A.-Salingaros.pdf) formalizes the coupling types ("one pattern contains or generalizes another smaller-scale pattern"; complementary, overlapping, alternative, structurally similar) and notes the graph "has multiple tops and horizontal connections," i.e. it is a scale-indexed network rather than a tree, and composes naturally across scales because "larger patterns utilize these groupings and also generate new properties that are not present in the component patterns."

**Takeaway for intent specs.** Every framework above agrees on the "why up, how down" reading and on many-to-many links except Axiomatic Design. Only QFD, Axiomatic Design, C-FBS, and pattern languages have a built-in composition rule across scales; the AH literature does nesting by convention (boundary objects, systems of systems) and its strongest critic considers the absence of a composition rule a genuine gap. Leveson's own position that level count and content are domain-specific is directly inherited from Vicente & Rasmussen (1992).
